Masked reconstruction: learning by filling in gaps

Week 6, clip 1 of 3. This is the narration of the clip, which an AI-generated voice reads in the narrated version; the silent version shows the same steps with on-screen captions only.

We start with a single lead ECG recording, ten seconds long. Nobody has labeled it with a diagnosis, so all we have is the raw signal.

We cut the signal into one second patches. Just like words in a sentence, each patch becomes one token for a transformer encoder.

Then we hide most of the patches at random. Here, six of the ten patches are masked out.

The encoder sees only the four visible patches, and turns each of them into an embedding vector.

A small decoder takes these embeddings, plus a placeholder mask token for every hidden patch, and predicts the missing waveform.

The loss is the mean squared error between the prediction and the true signal, computed only on the patches that were hidden.

Over many training steps, the predictions improve. To fill the gaps well, the encoder has to learn the structure of a heartbeat: the P wave, the QRS complex, and the T wave.

After pretraining, the decoder has done its job and is discarded. We keep the encoder, which now produces useful representations of ECG signals.

The same recipe works for pathology. A masked autoencoder hides most patches of an H&E tile, here 12 of 16, and learns to reconstruct the missing pixels.

BME 6938, University of Florida, Spring 2027. Taught by Dr. Parisa Rashidi.
