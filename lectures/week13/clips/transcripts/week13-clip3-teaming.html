Humans and models together

Week 13, clip 3 of 3. This is the narration of the clip, which an AI-generated voice reads in the narrated version; the silent version shows the same steps with on-screen captions only.

Put a clinician and a model together and there are two obvious rules: flag if either flags, or flag only if both do. Say the clinician catches 80% of cases and the model 75. Which rule is better depends on something nobody measures.

Assume their errors are independent. Of a hundred real cases, both catch 60, the clinician alone 20, the model alone 15, and 5 are missed by both. Either-flags reaches 0.95, and both-flags falls to 0.60.

Now assume the model misses whatever the clinician misses. Both catch 75, the clinician alone 5, and 20 are missed by both. Either-flags falls to 0.80, which is the clinician alone, so the model adds nothing. Both-flags rises to 0.75. The same two sensitivities, opposite conclusions.

And people are not rules. A confident score can talk a clinician out of a correct judgement, and at one true case in every eight alerts the rational response is to stop reading them. Goh and colleagues measured exactly this: the model alone scored 92%, and the physicians using it scored 76, against 74 without.

Ambient intelligence watches the room instead of waiting for someone to type. 16 depth sensors detected hand hygiene at 92.1% sensitivity, better than the human observers at 85.2. But that came from only 88 events, so read the interval. In an ICU, 7 sensors detected mobility at 87%, and counted the staff involved correctly only 69% of the time.

Sensing a care space is a governance question first. Depth sensing keeps identifiable images out of the room, which helps, but it does not settle who consents, who sees the data, or how long it is kept, and the staff are being observed too. Validate locally, watch it after go-live, and measure the team rather than the model.
