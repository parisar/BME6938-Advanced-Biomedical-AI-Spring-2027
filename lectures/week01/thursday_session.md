# Week 1, Thursday January 14: how to read a paper, and a model session

The only Thursday with no student-led sessions. Period 7 is the instructor's walk-through of how papers are read,
presented, and critiqued in this course. Period 8 is a model paper session on Moor et al., run the way student
sessions will run, so that the format is concrete before anyone has to do it.

Paper: Moor et al., [Foundation models for generalist medical artificial intelligence](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-05881-4),
Nature, 2023. Everyone reads it; nobody writes a reading response for it.

## Period 7 (1:55-2:45): Lecture 1-2

Slides: `1-2 Reading, Presenting, and Critiquing Papers`. The session covers how to read a paper in passes, how the
20-minute presentation is structured, what a critique is and is not, and a walk-through of both rubrics
([presentations/rubric.md](../../presentations/rubric.md)).

End the period by naming the two Week 2 papers and asking for volunteer groups, since those groups get the shortest
notice of the term.

## Period 8 (3:00-3:50): model paper session

Run it on the clock the students will face, and say so as you go.

| Time | What happens |
|---|---|
| 3:00-3:20 | Presentation of Moor et al. (20 min), given by the instructor in the student format |
| 3:20-3:35 | Critique (15 min), also by the instructor, out loud and deliberately |
| 3:35-3:49 | Discussion with the whole class |

### What the presentation half should model

- The problem and the stakes before any method: what a generalist medical model is meant to do that task-specific
  models cannot.
- The three capabilities the paper proposes (flexible interactions, flexible tasks, flexible modalities) and the
  use cases built on them.
- What kind of paper this is. It is a perspective, not a study: there are no experiments, and the figures
  illustrate proposals rather than report results. Saying this plainly teaches students to identify the paper type
  before judging the evidence.
- Slides in the presenter's own words and own figures, which is what the rubric asks for.

### What the critique half should model

- Restating the claims accurately first, including the distinction between "this is technically foreseeable" and
  "this will help patients".
- Asking what evidence a perspective can carry, and which claims would need a trial to support.
- Specific, prioritized concerns rather than a list: validation of a model that can be asked to do any task,
  verification of its outputs, data access and privacy, and who is accountable when a general model is used off
  label.
- Ending with something constructive: what study would test the central claim.

### Discussion questions to hold in reserve

- If a model can be asked to do any task, what does validation even mean?
- Which of the paper's use cases is closest to being deployable today, and what is missing for it?
- The paper is from 2023. Which parts have aged well, and which have not?
